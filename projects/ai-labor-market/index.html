AI Exposure & the North Carolina Labor Market

01 — PROBLEM DEFINITION

Research Question

How do employment, wages, and projected employment growth vary across occupations with different levels of AI exposure in major North Carolina metropolitan labor markets?

When most people think of AI adoption or exposure in the workplace, they often associate it with job loss. As someone who has worked heavily in the technology field while having friends whose occupations range from teachers to dental professionals to graphic designers, I am often asked: “Do you think AI can do my job?”

That question became much more real when a close friend working as a graphic designer was laid off and her work was replaced by AI. It made me question whether occupations with greater exposure to AI actually face weaker employment prospects.

This analysis examines five occupations across four major North Carolina metropolitan areas using 2025 Bureau of Labor Statistics data and national employment projections through 2035.

03 — CURRENT LABOR MARKET

Where Are These Jobs Today?

Employment varies considerably across both occupation and metropolitan area. Charlotte-Concord-Gastonia has the largest combined employment across the five selected occupations, followed by Raleigh-Cary.

Elementary School Teachers represent the largest occupation in each market, while Information Security Analyst employment is considerably more concentrated in Charlotte and Raleigh than in Greensboro-High Point.

04 — WAGES

Occupation Matters More Than Geography

Information Security Analysts have the highest median wages across all four metropolitan areas, reaching approximately $143,640 in Raleigh-Cary.

Raleigh-Cary also has the highest average median wage across the five occupations at approximately $84,222, compared with approximately $72,312 in Greensboro-High Point.

05 — EMPLOYMENT & WAGES

Different Occupations, Different Labor Markets

Employment size and wages do not move together uniformly. Elementary School Teachers have the largest employment counts but comparatively moderate wages, while Information Security Analysts have much smaller employment levels and substantially higher wages.

Breaking the analysis into individual metropolitan areas also shows that workers in the same occupation encounter different labor-market conditions depending on where they work.

06 — AI EXPOSURE & THE FUTURE

Higher AI Exposure Does Not Automatically Mean Fewer Jobs

The relationship between AI exposure and projected employment growth is not consistent across the five occupations.

The strongest contrast occurs among occupations with Very High AI exposure. Graphic Designers are projected to decline by approximately 1.7%, while Information Security Analysts are projected to grow by approximately 21.0% between 2025 and 2035.

Dental Hygienists with Low exposure are projected to grow 8.0%, Electricians with Moderate exposure by 9.2%, and Elementary School Teachers with High exposure are projected to remain relatively stable at −0.4%.

KEY TAKEAWAY

AI exposure alone is not sufficient to explain an occupation's projected employment outlook.

07 — CONCLUSION

So, Are the Jobs Safe?

After looking at the data, I have something important to communicate to my friends: I don't know if your jobs are safe, but AI exposure may not play as large a role as we initially thought.

Occupations with identical or similar AI exposure can have very different wages, employment concentrations, and projected growth. AI exposure appears to be one piece of a much larger labor-market picture involving the nature of the work, labor demand, geography, skills, and broader economic conditions.

This analysis does not establish that AI causes employment growth or decline. BLS AI exposure measures the relative potential for AI to interact with occupational tasks, while employment projections incorporate many other economic and labor-market factors.

08 — LIMITATIONS & REFLECTION

What the Data Cannot Tell Us

This analysis includes only five occupations and four metropolitan areas and should not be generalized to the entire North Carolina workforce.

Current employment and wage estimates are specific to the selected North Carolina metropolitan areas, while the 2025–2035 employment projections are national.

Most importantly, AI exposure is not the same as actual AI adoption or job displacement. It measures the relative potential for AI to interact with occupational tasks.

In a future extension of this project, I would expand the number of occupations and labor markets, examine changes across multiple years, and incorporate measures of actual workplace AI adoption.
