01 — PROBLEM DEFINITION

Predicting AI Use Across Businesses

Can machine-learning models predict the percentage of businesses within a sector and employee-size group that report using AI, not using AI, or not knowing whether they use AI?

Artificial intelligence adoption is becoming an increasingly important part of business decision-making. However, adoption is not uniform across businesses. Industry, business size, economic conditions, and other characteristics may all be associated with whether businesses report using AI.

The goal of this project was to build a machine-learning prediction system that estimates the distribution of AI-use responses across business groups. Rather than predicting a single yes-or-no outcome, the model predicts three percentages: Yes, No, and Do not know.

Because these three predictions represent a complete distribution, the final predictions were validated so that each business group totals exactly 100 percent.

02 — BACKGROUND & CONTEXT

Why Business AI Adoption Matters

AI adoption can affect how businesses approach productivity, staffing, pricing, customer service, forecasting, and other operational decisions. Understanding differences in adoption across industries and business sizes can therefore provide useful context for understanding how quickly AI is becoming integrated into the business environment.

This project uses information from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS). The survey provides information about current business conditions and includes questions concerning businesses' use of artificial intelligence.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

A model that predicts AI adoption accurately does not automatically establish why a business adopted AI. Prediction and causal explanation are different analytical goals.

03 — DATA DESCRIPTION

Business Conditions and AI Adoption

Data Source

The analysis uses data from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS). The survey provides business-level information about economic conditions, expectations, operations, and technology use.

Unit of Analysis

Prediction Structure

04 — DATA UNDERSTANDING & EXPLORATION

Understanding the Prediction Problem

Before modeling, the data were examined for missing values, categorical variables, numerical variables, duplicate groups, and the distribution of the three AI-use outcomes.

Sector and employee-size group were retained as categorical predictors. Numerical survey variables describing business conditions were used as additional predictors.

Because the publicly available BTOS data are published as aggregated survey estimates rather than business-level microdata, this analysis treats each sector–employment-size combination within the selected survey period as one observational unit. Some Census estimates were suppressed and therefore unavailable for analysis. These suppressed values were converted to missing values during preprocessing rather than treated as reported zero values.

Categorical Features

The model includes sector and employee-size categories. These variables allow the model to capture differences between industries and business sizes.

Numerical Features

Thirty-eight numerical predictors describe business conditions and survey responses, including measures related to prices, employment, time, operations, and other reported conditions.

A major consideration during exploration was that the target is not a conventional single-label classification problem. Each group has three percentages that must collectively represent the complete distribution of AI-use responses.

This led to treating the task as a multi-output regression problem rather than forcing the observations into a single categorical label.

11 — LIMITATIONS, ETHICS & REFLECTION

What the Model Can and Cannot Tell Us

Limited Sample

The final modeling dataset contains only 89 sector and employee-size groups. This relatively small number of observations limits how confidently the model can be generalized beyond the groups represented in the data.

Prediction vs. Causation

Feature importance and predictive accuracy do not establish that any particular business characteristic causes AI adoption. The model identifies patterns useful for prediction.

Survey Limitations

Survey-based measures depend on how businesses interpret questions and report their conditions. Measurement error or differences in reporting may affect model performance.

Ethical Considerations

Predictions about AI adoption could be misused if they are treated as definitive assessments of individual businesses. A business predicted to have a high probability of AI adoption should not be assumed to actually use AI.

These predictions should not be used as the sole basis for employment, lending, regulatory, or other high-impact decisions. Model errors can cause AI adoption to be overestimated or underestimated within particular sectors or employee-size groups.

One of the most important lessons from this project is that strong training performance does not automatically mean that a model will perform equally well on new data.

The Decision Tree had higher training error and also produced substantially higher cross-validated error, while the final model generalized better across the five validation folds.

The project also demonstrates why model interpretation matters. Feature importance can reveal which characteristics are useful for prediction, but those relationships should not be treated as causal explanations without additional research.

12 — CONCLUSION

Final Takeaways

FINAL RESULT

The final machine-learning model substantially outperformed the baseline and the Decision Tree, achieving the lowest cross-validated prediction error.

The baseline model produced an MAE of 9.5053, while the final model reduced training MAE to 1.6784. The Decision Tree produced a training MAE of 2.6447.

More importantly, five-fold cross-validation produced an MAE of 5.1617 for the final model compared with 6.7027 for the Decision Tree. This represents approximately 23% lower cross-validated prediction error.

The results suggest that the final model provides the strongest predictive approach among the models tested for estimating the distribution of AI-use responses across the available business groups.

At the same time, the model should be viewed as a predictive tool, not as evidence that the identified features cause businesses to adopt or avoid AI.

What This Analysis Does Not Establish

This analysis does not establish that any particular business characteristic causes AI adoption. The model identifies patterns that improve prediction within the available data, but causal conclusions would require a different research design.
