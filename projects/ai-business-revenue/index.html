01 — PROBLEM DEFINITION

Research Question

Can a machine-learning model predict the percentage of businesses reporting AI use based on business characteristics such as industry sector, employee size, and other survey measures?

Artificial intelligence adoption has become an important indicator of how businesses are changing the way they operate. However, AI adoption is not distributed evenly across businesses. Industry, firm size, business conditions, and other characteristics can all be associated with whether businesses report using AI.

This project uses the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS) to develop a machine-learning approach for predicting AI-use percentages across business groups.

Rather than predicting a simple yes-or-no outcome for an individual business, the model predicts the percentage distribution of three survey responses within each business group: Yes, No, and Do not know.

02 — BACKGROUND & CONTEXT

Why Business AI Adoption Matters

Measuring AI adoption is important because businesses do not adopt emerging technologies at the same rate. Recent Census Bureau research using BTOS data shows meaningful differences in AI use across industries and firm sizes. The Census Bureau's current BTOS AI data also tracks how businesses use AI and how adoption varies by business characteristics.

Earlier BTOS research found that AI use was concentrated in particular sectors and was more common among larger businesses. More recent research continues to show that firm size and industry are important dimensions of AI diffusion.

The broader research literature also suggests that AI adoption is connected to complementary resources such as digital infrastructure, skills, management capabilities, and firm characteristics. This makes sector and employee size reasonable variables to consider when building a predictive model.

Why Machine Learning?

Traditional descriptive analysis can show differences in AI adoption across groups. A machine-learning model goes one step further by learning patterns from multiple variables and using those patterns to generate predictions for groups with known characteristics.

What the Model Is Not

This model is predictive rather than causal. A feature that is useful for prediction is not necessarily causing businesses to adopt AI. Feature importance should therefore be interpreted as evidence of predictive contribution, not proof of a causal relationship.

03 — DATA DESCRIPTION

Business Trends and Outlook Survey

The primary data source for this project is the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS). BTOS is an ongoing survey designed to provide timely information about business conditions, expectations, revenue, employment, and other characteristics of U.S. businesses.

The Census Bureau has also incorporated AI-related questions into BTOS to measure business use of artificial intelligence. The survey provides a useful source for examining differences in AI adoption across industries and firm-size categories.

Unit of Analysis

The modeling data are organized around business groups rather than individual survey respondents. Each row represents a unique combination of Sector and Empsize.

Predictor Information

  • Business sector
  • Employee-size category
  • Additional numeric survey measures describing business conditions
  • Additional categorical or encoded business characteristics used by the model

Target Variables

The model predicts three related outcomes:

  • Percentage reporting Yes
  • Percentage reporting No
  • Percentage reporting Do not know
TARGET STRUCTURE

The three predicted percentages are constrained to represent a complete distribution. For every group, Pred_Yes + Pred_No + Pred_Do_not_know = 100.

The final prediction table contains 89 groups. Validation checks found zero duplicate groups, and every prediction row totals exactly 100.0.

05 — DATA PREPARATION & FEATURE SELECTION

Preparing the Data for Modeling

Before training the model, the data were transformed into a format suitable for machine learning. Numerical and categorical variables were handled separately so that the final estimator could work with both types of information.

Categorical Variables

Categorical business characteristics, including sector and employee-size categories, were encoded so that they could be incorporated into the machine-learning pipeline.

Numerical Variables

Numeric survey measures were retained as quantitative predictors and processed through the modeling pipeline.

Feature Selection

Features were selected based on their availability in the modeling data and their potential relevance to business AI adoption. The model's feature-importance results were then used to examine which variables contributed most strongly to prediction.

Leakage Prevention

Preprocessing and model fitting were structured so that the predictive workflow could be evaluated without using the target values from the evaluation process as input features.

IMPORTANT MODELING DECISION

Because the three target percentages must describe one complete distribution, predictions were validated after modeling to ensure that each group remained interpretable as a 100% response distribution.

9.5053 Baseline MAE

BASELINE

Reference Prediction

The baseline provides a simple benchmark for prediction error. It does not attempt to learn complex relationships between business characteristics and AI adoption.

FINAL MODEL

Gradient Boosting

Gradient Boosting was used as the final predictive model because it can capture nonlinear relationships and interactions among business characteristics without requiring those relationships to be specified manually.

The final model reduced MAE from 9.5053 to 2.1014, an absolute improvement of 7.4039 points and a relative improvement of 77.89%.

This means the final model's average absolute prediction error was substantially smaller than the error produced by the baseline.

11 — RESULTS & CONCLUSION

What the Model Tells Us

MY TAKEAWAY

Business characteristics contain meaningful predictive information about AI adoption. The Gradient Boosting model reduced prediction error by 77.89% compared with the baseline, but cross-validation shows that performance on unseen data is more difficult than the final diagnostic MAE alone suggests.

The strongest evidence comes from the comparison with the baseline. The baseline MAE of 9.5053 fell to 2.1014 for the final model, representing a 7.4039-point reduction in absolute error.

The model also performed substantially better than the baseline for each target category. Prediction error for the Yes category fell by 78.36%, error for No fell by 80.24%, and error for Do not know fell by 69.98%.

At the same time, the cross-validated MAE of 4.9874 provides an important qualification. A model that performs very well on one evaluation output may perform less accurately when repeatedly tested on held-out data. This difference is one reason validation is essential in machine learning.

What This Analysis Does Not Establish

The model does not demonstrate that any particular business characteristic causes AI adoption. Feature importance identifies predictive usefulness, not causal influence.

The results also should not be interpreted as an individual-business prediction tool. The model operates on grouped survey data and predicts percentages for combinations of business characteristics.

12 — LIMITATIONS, ETHICS & REFLECTION

Limitations & Reflection

Group-Level Data

The model predicts aggregated response percentages for business groups. It should not be interpreted as predicting whether a particular individual company will adopt AI.

Survey Measurement

BTOS is a survey-based source. Reported responses depend on how businesses understand and answer the survey questions, and survey measures may not capture every dimension of AI adoption.

Generalization

The model was developed using the available dataset and 89 prediction groups. Performance may change when applied to a different period, population, or distribution of business characteristics.

Potential Sources of Bias

AI adoption may differ systematically across industries, business sizes, regions, and types of organizations. If some groups are underrepresented or measured differently, the model may learn those patterns unevenly. Survey nonresponse and differences in how businesses interpret AI-related questions may also affect the underlying target measurements.

Consequences of Incorrect Predictions

Incorrect predictions could lead a user to overestimate or underestimate AI adoption in a particular business group. This could be especially problematic if predictions were used to make investment, workforce, policy, or resource-allocation decisions without considering uncertainty and the limitations of the model.

Reflection

One of the most important lessons from this project was that strong predictive performance and trustworthy generalization are not the same thing. The model dramatically improved on the baseline, but cross-validation produced a higher MAE than the final diagnostic result.

The project also reinforced the importance of looking beyond a single performance score. Examining target-level error, row-level error, and feature importance provided a much better understanding of how the model was behaving.

If I continued this project, I would compare additional machine- learning algorithms, perform more systematic hyperparameter tuning, investigate the source of the largest prediction errors, and test whether the model remains stable across different BTOS time periods.
