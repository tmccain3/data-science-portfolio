01 — PROBLEM DEFINITION

Predicting AI Use Across Businesses

Can machine-learning models predict the percentage of businesses within a sector and employee-size group that report using AI, not using AI, or not knowing whether they use AI?

Artificial intelligence adoption is becoming an increasingly important part of business decision-making. However, adoption is not uniform across businesses. Industry, business size, economic conditions, and other characteristics may all be associated with whether businesses report using AI.

The goal of this project was to build a machine-learning prediction system that estimates the distribution of AI-use responses across business groups. Rather than predicting a single yes-or-no outcome, the model predicts three percentages: businesses reporting Yes, No, and Do not know.

Because the three predictions represent a distribution, the final predictions were constrained so that each business group totals 100 percent.

02 — BACKGROUND & CONTEXT

Why Business AI Adoption Matters

AI adoption can affect how businesses approach productivity, staffing, pricing, customer service, forecasting, and other operational decisions. Understanding differences in adoption across industries and business sizes can therefore provide useful context for understanding how quickly AI is becoming integrated into the business environment.

This project uses information from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS). The survey provides information about current business conditions and includes questions concerning businesses' use of artificial intelligence.

The machine-learning objective is predictive rather than causal. The purpose is to identify patterns that can improve predictions of AI-use distributions, not to claim that any individual feature causes a business to adopt AI.

IMPORTANT DISTINCTION

A model that predicts AI adoption accurately does not automatically establish why a business adopted AI. Prediction and causal explanation are different analytical goals.

03 — DATA DESCRIPTION

Business Conditions and AI Adoption

Data Source

The analysis uses data from the U.S. Census Bureau's Business Trends and Outlook Survey (BTOS).

The survey provides business-level information about economic conditions, expectations, operations, and technology use.

Unit of Analysis

The final modeling table contains 89 unique combinations of sector and employee-size categories.

Each row represents one sector/employee-size group and contains the observed percentage distribution of businesses reporting Yes, No, or Do not know regarding AI use.

Target Variables

  • Target_Yes: percentage of businesses reporting AI use.
  • Target_No: percentage of businesses reporting that they do not use AI.
  • Target_Do_not_know: percentage of businesses reporting that they do not know whether they use AI.

These three targets sum to 100 percent for every group. This structure was preserved when generating the final predictions.

04 — DATA UNDERSTANDING & EXPLORATION

Understanding the Prediction Problem

Before modeling, the data were examined for missing values, categorical variables, numerical variables, duplicate groups, and the distribution of the three AI-use outcomes.

Sector and employee-size group were retained as categorical predictors. Numerical survey variables describing business conditions were used as additional predictors.

Categorical Features

The model includes sector and employee-size categories. These variables allow the model to capture differences between industries and business sizes.

Numerical Features

Thirty-eight numerical predictors describe business conditions and survey responses. These include measures related to prices, employment, time, operations, and other reported business conditions.

A major consideration during exploration was that the target is not a conventional single-label classification problem. Each group has three percentages that must collectively represent the complete distribution of AI-use responses.

This led to treating the task as a multi-output regression problem rather than forcing the observations into a single categorical label.

08 — MODEL EVALUATION

Cross-Validation and Final Model Selection

Five-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate how well each model generalized across different subsets of the 89 observations. Mean absolute error (MAE) was used because it expresses the average magnitude of prediction error in the same percentage-point units as the target variables.

Model Training MAE 5-Fold CV MAE
Final Model 1.6784 5.1617
Decision Tree 2.6447 6.7027
MODEL SELECTION

The final model achieved a cross-validated MAE of 5.1617 compared with 6.7027 for the Decision Tree — approximately 23% lower cross-validated prediction error.

The final model was therefore retained as the preferred model. The decision is supported by both its lower training error and, more importantly, its lower cross-validated error.

10 — ERROR ANALYSIS

Where Predictions Were Most Difficult

Error analysis was performed at the individual sector and employee-size group level. Across the 89 groups, the mean row-level MAE was 1.6784 and the median row-level MAE was 1.4979.

The largest observed errors occurred in several groups where the model shifted predictions between the Yes, No, and Do not know categories. For example, the largest error occurred for sector 56, employee-size group B, where the target percentages were 16.6% Yes, 74.6% No, and 8.8% Do not know, while the model predicted approximately 22.1%, 69.0%, and 9.4%, respectively.

INTERPRETATION

The model generally captures the overall distribution of AI adoption, but prediction errors remain for individual sector/employee-size combinations.

11 — LIMITATIONS, ETHICS & REFLECTION

What the Model Can and Cannot Tell Us

Limited Sample

The final modeling dataset contains only 89 sector and employee-size groups. This relatively small number of observations limits how confidently the model can be generalized beyond the groups represented in the data.

Prediction vs. Causation

Feature importance and predictive accuracy do not establish that any particular business characteristic causes AI adoption. The model identifies patterns useful for prediction.

Survey Limitations

Survey-based measures depend on how businesses interpret questions and report their conditions. Measurement error or differences in reporting may affect model performance.

Ethical Considerations

Predictions about AI adoption could be misused if they are treated as definitive assessments of individual businesses. A business predicted to have a high probability of AI adoption should not be assumed to actually use AI, and predictions should not be used as the sole basis for employment, lending, regulatory, or other high-impact decisions.

Model errors also affect different groups differently. Because the target represents percentages across business groups, errors can cause the model to overestimate or underestimate the prevalence of AI use within particular sectors or employee-size categories.

One of the most important lessons from this project is that strong training performance does not automatically mean that a model will perform equally well on new data. The Decision Tree had a higher training error and also produced substantially higher cross-validated error, while the final model generalized better across the five validation folds.

The project also demonstrates why model interpretation matters. Feature importance can reveal which characteristics are useful for prediction, but those relationships should not be treated as causal explanations without additional research.

12 — CONCLUSION

Final Takeaways

FINAL RESULT

The final machine-learning model substantially outperformed the baseline and the Decision Tree, achieving the lowest cross-validated prediction error.

The baseline model produced an MAE of 9.5053, while the final model reduced training MAE to 1.6784. The Decision Tree produced a training MAE of 2.6447.

More importantly, five-fold cross-validation produced an MAE of 5.1617 for the final model compared with 6.7027 for the Decision Tree. This represents approximately 23% lower cross-validated prediction error.

The results suggest that the final model provides the strongest predictive approach among the models tested for estimating the distribution of AI-use responses across the available business groups.

At the same time, the model should be viewed as a predictive tool, not as evidence that the identified features cause businesses to adopt or avoid AI.
